A procedural world

A valley, its colouring, a scatter of rocks and a photographic sky, generated from code and a single image rather than modelled by hand.

Overview Source walkthrough Recap

Overview The big picture

The valley is a function

The ground isn't a model file. It's a function: give it an x and z position, and it returns the height of the ground there. At start-up the renderer samples that function on a 201 × 201 grid of points one metre apart, joins neighbouring points into triangles, and uploads the result as an ordinary mesh. The GPU never knows it was generated.

The same function is used everywhere the ground matters: to stand the hut, rocks and cubes on it, to keep the camera 1.8 m above it, and to plant every blade of grass (Chapter 14). Because they all ask the same question, nothing floats or sinks.

The height is built from layers:

  • Gentle rolling ground, up to about 2 m, from value noise: smooth pseudo-random bumps, made by blending random values placed on a grid, at three different scales (waves roughly 55, 21 and 8 m long), often called octaves.
  • Hills around the edge that rise from 36 m out, peak at 82 m, and fall back to the base level at the edge of the 200 m patch, so the playable centre feels enclosed.
  • A flat pad under the hut, blended smoothly into the surrounding ground.

Colour without textures

The terrain has no texture image. Each vertex gets a colour from the same kind of noise: dark, fertile soil where grass will be dense, warm dry earth in the bare patches, and grey near rocks and on steep slopes. Its fragment shader then adds fine, centimetre-scale grit and pebbles, also generated from noise, so the ground holds up close to the camera without a large texture.

Rocks and sky

Sixteen hand-placed spots each get a cluster of three rocks, chosen from five imported models and rotated and sized by a fixed recipe, so the world is identical every run. The grass thins out around each cluster.

The sky is a single 360° photograph (an equirectangular panorama, like a world map wrapped around you). It's drawn first each frame with a trick: one triangle large enough to cover the whole screen, whose fragment shader works out which direction each pixel looks in and reads the matching point of the panorama. Because it ignores the camera's position, the sky stays infinitely far away however far you walk.

Deeper dive

Settings, and a height function

TerrainSettings is a small Copy struct, passed by value everywhere. The natural height combines three noise octaves into a landform, then adds the valley wall:

The "ridge" term folds the medium octave around its midpoint, which turns soft bumps into occasional shoulders and gullies. The wall is the product of a rising and a falling smooth step, which gives a rounded crest. Its distance uses x⁴ + z⁴ rather than x² + z², which bends the circle towards the square shape of the mesh. Inside the pad radius, the height is fixed at whatever the natural ground would be at the pad's centre, rather than zero, so the hut sits on a gentle shelf instead of in a dug-out bowl.

Noise from a hash

Value noise needs a repeatable random value at every integer grid point. That comes from a hash: mixing the coordinates' bits until they look random:

value_noise looks up the four grid points around a position and blends them with a smooth step in each direction, giving hills with no visible grid. (A smooth step is an S-shaped curve from 0 to 1 that eases in and out, used throughout this file for soft transitions.)

Rust note · overflow is checked

In C, unsigned multiplication silently wraps around. In Rust, ordinary arithmetic that overflows panics in debug builds, which catches a whole class of bugs. Hash functions want wrap-around, so they say so explicitly with wrapping_mul and wrapping_add. The as casts (x as u32) reinterpret or convert numbers explicitly; Rust never converts between numeric types implicitly.

Building the mesh

Each vertex samples the height function, then samples it again one cell either side in x and in z. Those central differences give the slope, and from the slope come a smooth normal, a tangent along +X and a bitangent from their cross product (the surface's local axes, which normal-mapped shading needs). So the lighting is smooth even though the mesh is made of flat triangles. A second loop emits two triangles per cell, wound counter-clockwise when seen from above, so their front faces point up (Chapter 7).

The vertex colour comes from the grass density at that spot, whether it's near a rock, and how steep it is:

grass_density_at is public because the grass generator (Chapter 14) samples the same function into a coarse map, so dense grass always stands on dark soil. The rock clearing is the minimum, over all sixteen clusters, of a smooth step around each cluster's clearing_radius.

Detail in the terrain shader

terrain.vert is a plain transform that also passes the world position along, and terrain.frag starts from that vertex colour and adds detail at scales the one-metre mesh can't represent, all from noise and hashes of the world position. The pebbles are the most interesting part:

The ground is divided into 20 cm cells. A hash decides whether each cell has a pebble (30% do), where in the cell it sits, how big it is and what shade. fwidth measures how much the distance changes from one pixel to the next and widens the pebble's edge to match, so pebbles soften rather than shimmer as they shrink into the distance. The lighting that follows has the same structure as Chapter 6's, including its own copy of the shadow lookup.

Rock clusters

Each anchor in ROCK_CLUSTERS (a constant array in terrain.rs) has a position, rotation, scale and starting variant. cluster_members derives three stones from it: a main stone at the anchor, and two smaller ones placed at angles nudged by sin and cos of the golden ratio times the cluster's index. That's deterministic "randomness": every cluster looks different, but the same on every run. The three variants step two entries along the five-model list, wrapping round, so each cluster mixes shapes. When placed (Chapter 12), each stone is also sunk by 7% of its size so it looks embedded. A test checks that all five models get used.

The rock models come from two asset packs, each sharing one texture atlas (one image holding the textures for several models), so all five share just two materials. Their vertex shader, rock.vert, is a plain transform; their fragment shader deliberately mutes the atlas's colours, keeping its brightness as the surface colour (albedo), so the packs look like the same stone:

The sky

SkyRenderer borrows its panorama from the material manager's texture cache, so the image joins the start-up upload batch (Chapter 5). It gets its own sampler, though:

The sampler and view go into a DescriptorImageInfo, which draw pushes as set 0 each frame, the same way materials push their textures (Chapter 8). Its pipeline is the new_background variant from Chapter 7: no depth test, no blending, no culling, and no vertex input at all. The three corners are built in the vertex shader from gl_VertexIndex:

The triangle's corners at (−1, −1), (3, −1) and (−1, 3) cover the whole −1..1 screen with one triangle, which is a little cheaper than two triangles meeting along a diagonal. For each corner, the inverse of projection × view turns a point on the far plane back into a world-space direction. The view has its translation zeroed first, which is what makes the sky infinitely distant:

The fragment shader converts the interpolated direction to longitude and latitude, the panorama's two texture coordinates:

Coming from OpenGL

The OpenGL classic was a cube map drawn on a cube around the camera with depth writes off. The idea here is the same, with a panorama instead of six faces and a fullscreen triangle instead of a cube. A draw with no vertex buffers was possible in OpenGL too, but needed an empty VAO bound; in Vulkan it simply means a pipeline with an empty vertex input state.

Recap

  • The terrain is a deterministic height function (noise at three scales, a valley wall, a flat hut pad), sampled on a 201 × 201 grid and uploaded as an ordinary mesh. Everything placed on the ground asks the same function.
  • Normals come from central differences. Colour comes from grass density, rock proximity and slope, with grit and pebbles added in the fragment shader.
  • Sixteen anchors each yield three rocks from five shared models, by a fixed recipe; the grass clears around them.
  • The sky is one fullscreen triangle sampling a panorama by view direction, with translation removed so it stays infinitely far away.
